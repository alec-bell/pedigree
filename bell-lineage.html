Robert Bell of Chowan & Bertie Candidate
in Virginia by 10 Dec 1703, possibly years earlier — imported at Capt. Richard Covington’s charge, certified in Essex court (DOCUMENTED, adversary-confirmed [BELL-524/525]); his servant, freed c.1708–09 (identification with the Chowan man PROBABLE); on the Cashie, N.C., by 1719 — on a list taken by a constable between Salmon Creek and the Cashie, which the manuscript suggests is a TITHABLES list, not a freeholders’ roll: presence documented, tenure not (#268); d. intestate Bertie Co., mid-1738
A fully documented man — and now, at PROBABLE grade (24 Aug [BELL-508], adversary-confirmed), a man with a beginning: a servant of Col. Richard Covington of Essex County, Virginia, who sued his master for his freedom clothes and won — dismissed for non-appearance and then heard and won in the same sitting, 10 May 1708, with Covington himself on the bench. He had already received “a Shirt & a pair of Briches”; the court ordered the rest — “a pair of [shoes], a pair of Stockens, a Kersey Coat & an hatt with costs.” (The word shoes sits under a film blemish and is a reconstruction, not read text — #256.) A dying neighbour left him everything he had (£12 14s 0d) in 1711; he took the executorship jointly bound with Henry Woodnot, and was still suing in Essex court on 14 Mar 1712/13 (#254); by 1719 he was a freeholder in the Carolina precinct where Woodnot — fleeing his debts — had gone before him. He never patented an acre in either county (nclandgrants: no Bell in Chowan at all; Bertie’s earliest Bell grant is 1739, the year after he died) — he bought, twice, from neighbours. Woodnot’s 1718 will names Robert’s wife Anne, a widow Ferguson. He bought rather than patented — 200 ac on the Cashie (1721), the 640-ac Rose “Bucklesberry” tract (1726) — sold 206 ac to Governor Charles Eden in person (#267 — the file long said 606) (the clerk rendering his mark as “R B”), and was left estates by four dying men in eighteen years. His children named themselves in their own 1738/9 receipts: George (eldest), John, Archibald, Penelope; his grave is reserved for ever in his son’s 1758 deed: “Except the Graves as before mentioned.” 29 Aug 2026 (#257): the “he never patented” oddity this card used to carry is withdrawn. Robert Beverley, writing in 1705, says a freed servant’s right to fifty acres was “no great Privilege, for any one may have as good a right for a piece of Eight” — from 1699 the colony sold fifty acres for five shillings over the counter. Buying rather than patenting says nothing about a man’s means or his origin. What is still carried openly: a freedman finding £30 for land twelve years on, and his missing boy Robert of 1711, unheard-of in Carolina.
What would prove it: He is the immigrant himself, and his importation is DOCUMENTED — adversary-confirmed [BELL-524/525]: his own master, Capt. Richard Covington, swore his importation in open Essex court on 10 Dec 1703 (with himself on the bench) — eleven heads, “Robt. Bell,” sixth of sixty-six transported persons on the Hay & Robinson patent, Essex County, 2 May 1705 — with a John Bell fifty-five names down the same certificate: two Bells on one patent — though the ORIGINAL LEAF, now read at the Library of Virginia’s own images [BELL-515], puts them forty-nine names apart: one importer, probably two certificates, the brothers-shape softened (#232) but the conjunction standing. Where in Britain they came FROM is the open question — and the silence is now DOCUMENTED, not assumed [BELL-516]: London’s port books for exactly his decade were deliberately destroyed, London’s servant bindings start 1718, Bristol’s register stops 1679 — and in the entire indexed emigration literature 1660–1720, exactly ONE Robert Bell arrives in Virginia: ours. The compiled “Sir Robert Bell of Norfolk” stories cite nothing — and Norfolk scores no higher than Lincolnshire on the surname’s own geography. 29 Aug 2026: the Border-counties/Ulster prior this page used to carry is WITHDRAWN (#247) — it reasoned from where the surname lives rather than from the channel a servant travelled. The Chesapeake servant stream drew on London, southern and central England and the West Country, and only thinly on the northern counties; the North-British migration to America does not open until 1717, fourteen years after Robert Bell was already in an Essex courtroom. His crossing is now dated c.1701–1703 and quantified: London owned 36 of the 48 ships of the 1703 Chesapeake fleet, counted in Whitehall two days before Covington swore him into the colony [BELL-539]. Five channels were closed at the record on 29 Aug — Scottish and Irish transportation, the Old Swan Lane servant trade, Covington as a recruiter, the cohort as a lever, and the naval-office returns, which have no passenger column and can never name him. What survives is inference from the people around him. Permanent caveat: a 1705 King & Queen patentee of the same name is unexcludable (burned county) but unsupported.
